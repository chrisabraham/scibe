Caucus

Caucus is computer-conferencing software: a system for holding many simultaneous, ongoing, written conversations on one machine. Its grammar is deliberately spare—Caucus keeps discussions, in its makers’ 1998 formulation, “to just two levels: conference items and participants’ responses within those items”. A host opens and tends a conference; a participant opens an item; responses follow in order, signed and dated. That is the whole machine, and it is the machine one half of this work was written in: ARTS WIRE ran its conferences on Caucus, and The Meta Network ran on it—Item 8, preserved at item8.org, is one of its items by name, item 8 of TMN’s artist conference.

One work, two grammars. Scibe was written simultaneously in two different conferencing grammars, and the difference is preserved on the face of every lexia. On the ARTS WIRE side, the writing stands as Caucus holds talk: a member opens an item, and the others answer in responses—signed, dated, appended in order beneath it. On The WELL side, the same work was posted in PicoSpan, which holds talk as topics within conferences. Each lexia in this edition keeps the header it was posted under—conference, writer, date—because the header is the grammar’s own bookkeeping: it tells a reader which room a piece of the work was spoken in, and in which of the two grammars. One hyperfiction, composed at the same time in an item-and-response system and in a topic system, is this entry’s reason for being told from this chair: Caucus is Item 8’s native room, but it is Scibe’s neighbour—and Scibe is the work that lived in both.

The ARTS WIRE half’s tenancy is not inference; it is listed. Screen Porch—the company that carried Caucus onto the web—printed its own product pages naming the communities using the software, and ArtsWire stands in that list, with The Metanet beside it. That listing is this entry’s centre of evidence: the room where half of Scibe was written ran on this software, on the software maker’s own say-so, in the software maker’s own catalogue.

Cousins, not coincidence. This edition’s editor, who wrote in both kinds of room, asked whether the likeness between TMN’s Caucus and the WELL’s conferencing was coincidence—“like chimps coming up with food washing concurrently on different islands”. The record answers no. Both systems descend from CONFER: its record names Caucus and PicoSpan alike among its progeny, and PicoSpan’s own record says it “tried to provide CONFER’s functionality while using the least amount of resources”. TMN itself began, in 1983, on CONFER II. And the cousins visited: ARTS WIRE’s community lived in Caucus rooms while its people also wrote on the WELL, and Scibe was composed in both at once. Documented descent, documented contact; the genuinely independent inventions belong to the generation before, at PLATO and EIES (see online conferencing).

Its maker is Charles Roth. At Wayne State University in the early 1980s he saw CONFER, Robert Parnes’s 1975 Michigan system, and—his words—“as soon as I saw the power of this, I wanted to write one that everybody could use”. The portable program he built through Camber-Roth, a division of his company Aule-Tek, was named Caucus; its first copy sold in 1986, and by 1990 it supported two full-time developers. Roth bought out the rights to Caucus in 1994, for most of his Aule-Tek stock.

In February 1995 Roth discovered the World Wide Web and, in a month, wrote CML—the Caucus Markup Language—and a web interface for the system. CML was more than a name for that conversion: Roth’s own Reference Guide defines it as a scripting language combining HTML tags with simple programming constructs and database functions, its interpreter “the core of the World Wide Web interface” to Caucus, and CaucusCare’s architecture description says every page the web system displayed was the output of a CML file—“a language like PHP or JSP or ASP, that describes what kind of data goes where on the page”. The conference interface itself was editable text: an early web templating language from the platform’s own author.

The system’s history was later taken down inside the system itself: in 2004, in a Caucus conference at CaucusCare, Lisa Kimball—a partner in Metasystems Design Group from 1984, and the founder of the Electronic Networking Association—interviewed Roth about the beginnings, meaning, in her words, “to capture some Caucus history here so everyone has more of an appreciation of how Caucus has evolved” (see the village). This entry’s account of Caucus rests on that interview and on Roth’s own history.

What followed the web conversion—the founding of Screen Porch in 1996 and Caucus’s web years, the forming of Caucus Systems in 1999, Roth’s layoff in 2000 and the company’s bankruptcy late in 2001, the return of the rights to Roth, and the software’s open-source afterlife under CaucusCare, through versions 5 and 5.1—is told in full in Item 8’s Caucus entry, where Caucus is the native room and the saga is at home.
