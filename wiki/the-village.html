The village

It took a village. The works in this edition were not published by platforms in the modern sense; they were hosted—by named people who founded the systems, wrote the software, tended the conferences, and kept the machines running. This roll names the principal ones, each with the role the public record supports. Where a role rests on this edition’s editor’s own recollection rather than a document, the entry says so. It leads with the keepers of this work’s own two rooms—ARTS WIRE and The WELL—because those are the rooms Scibe was written in.

Anne Focke, Seattle artist and arts administrator, proposed ARTS WIRE itself: at NYFA’s 1988 gathering on Orcas Island she put forward a virtual place for the arts of all kinds, and she first led what came of the proposal. Ted Berger, the New York Foundation for the Arts’ executive director, shared the vision and housed it—ARTS WIRE was a NYFA program, and Berger’s foundation was its roof. Joe Matuzak, a Michigan poet, directed ARTS WIRE from 1995. Anna Couey was its first network coordinator: she brought Judy Malloy onto the staff in 1993, and with Malloy she founded the Interactive Art Conference the same year; earlier, with Penny Boyer, she had been a first editor of Hotwire, the newsletter that became Arts Wire Current under David Green and then under Malloy, from 1996 to 2004. The staff never shared a building: they met as a virtual office, scattered across the country, and its steering committee met online too.

Inside the rooms, the keepers were also makers. Tim Collins and Reiko Goto served as virtual artists-in-residence in the Interactive Art Conference—the room where Scibe’s ARTS WIRE half was written. Composers Pauline Oliveros, David Mahler, and Douglas Cohen coordinated NewMusNet, one of ARTS WIRE’s first interest groups, from the summer of 1992. And inside The WELL, in 1986, Carl Loeffler and Fred Truck set up Art Com Electronic Network, the electronic exhibition space where Malloy published Uncle Roger.

The WELL’s own keepers stand at the depth their room earns on this site, since the WELL is the work’s surviving home. Stewart Brand and Larry Brilliant founded it in February 1985; Brand set its founding terms in the member agreement—“You Own Your Own Words”—and its conferences have been tended ever since by hosts, named members who guide the talk in named rooms. Judy Malloy, in her village capacity, belongs to both rooms at once: ARTS WIRE staff from 1993 and editor of its newsletter to 2004, founder of the WELL’s Arts conference, co-founder of INTERACTIVE—and the writer whose sickroom appeal both rooms answered. She has her own entry.

TMN’s principals, on this site, stand in brief. Frank L. Burns founded The Meta Network in 1983 and was president of Metasystems Design Group; the network was his “information age version of the think tank”. Scott Burns, his son, kept the machines: system administrator of everything at TMN, living on site (the editor’s recollection); the Caucus 2.7 manual’s worked example names him beside his father—“the notsoelder”—and Caucus 4.1’s Bonanza macros were partly based on customizations he originally implemented. Tom Mandel led Screen Porch, Caucus’s web-era company, as its president in the 4.0 release of 1998. Charles Roth wrote Caucus: he saw CONFER at Wayne State, sold Caucus’s first copy in 1986, and carried it to the web in 1995. Robert Parnes built CONFER at the University of Michigan in 1975, the ancestor both Caucus and PicoSpan descend from; TMN itself opened on his CONFER II. Marcus D. Watts wrote PicoSpan, the WELL’s conferencing software, for NETI. The fuller roll of the network’s own people stands at item8.org.

Lisa Kimball stands between the rooms. She became a partner in Metasystems Design Group in 1984, and in 1985 she founded the Electronic Networking Association, the field’s own professional association. Rheingold’s verdict on her, in The Virtual Community, was that it is “hard to find any significant CMC system in the world that doesn’t have a contribution from her”. And it was Kimball who, in 2004, interviewed Roth inside his own software about how Caucus began—the interview this site’s Caucus entry rests on. Her full institutional biography belongs with the network’s own people, and stands at Item 8’s village.

Under all of them stood the hosts themselves—the WELL’s conference hosts, TMN’s conference organizers, ARTS WIRE’s staff meeting as a virtual office across five cities—named people tending named rooms, present in the talk they tended. That, more than any software, is the village: the works were written in rooms somebody kept.

And the eight writers were villagers too—each of them writing, in 1994, from inside these rooms. Judy Malloy originated and orchestrated the work (see her entry). Tom Igoe, second in the byline, wrote from a borrowed apartment in Boston; two lexias carry his header. Tim Collins walked Carson Street in Pittsburgh with Reiko Goto beside him—a resident writing in a room he helped keep. Anna Couey wrote one lexia and, as coordinator and co-founder of INTERACTIVE, had built part of the room the work was written in. Valerie Gardiner crossed the country by car, posting the road into the sickroom; nine lexias carry her header. Joseph Wilson’s five lexias carry his header, from wherever in that world he stood. Doug Cohen, NewMusNet’s coordinator, wrote one lexia, signed “douglas — sternklang dogs”. And Chris Abraham stands in this roll at the weight the record gives him: third in the 1994 copyright line; fifteen lexias carry his header, and the work index counts his name in eighteen; in 1994 he wrote from a desk at PNI, four doors from MDG’s Suite 103. The descent is his, and it is plain: in 1997 he opened item 8 on The Meta Network to recreate this work—the sister edition at item8.org.
