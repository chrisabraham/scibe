Telnet, and the move to the web

Before the web, a community was a telephone number. The era’s directories of online services--the PDIAL lists that circulated among the newly connected--entered The Meta Network as dialup in area codes 703, 202, and 301, its services listed as “Caucus conferencing, email, shell, ftp, telnet, bbs”, at $20 a month plus a $15 signup fee; The WELL as a Sausalito number, $15 a month plus $2 an hour. You set your modem to the number, the distant machine answered with a carrier tone, and text came back at the speed of typing.

Telnet was the era’s other door: a protocol for sitting, in text, at a machine elsewhere. A period memoir of online life records the whole ritual in one sentence: “I simply type ‘telnet tmn.com’, and instantly I am logged in to the Meta Network and given a list of open conferences and topics.” Scibe’s narrator travels the same way inside the work: her second lexia follows her story as it telnets from machine to machine toward PARC. The word was ordinary then; the work uses it the way a later work would say “sent”.

The transition, when it came, came fast: Roth discovered the web in February 1995 and had a web interface for Caucus written within a month; ARTS WIRE, text-only from 1992, moved to the web in 1996; by 1998 Caucus needed only a browser; and the WELL is now reached by SSH or the web.

The road this entry has followed had two endings. The tmn.com addresses--the road to the ARTS WIRE copy of Scibe, served from TMN’s server in the web years--are dead: they no longer answer, and the Wayback Machine holds only the server’s error pages for them. The WELL’s road is still travelled: its conferences continue under their hosts, its PicoSpan rooms intact underneath, and Scibe still stands where its author put it in 1994. One road ended with the world that built it; the other is the same road, still open.
