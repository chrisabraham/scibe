scibe> acknowledgements — thanks, by name, at length

A book ends by naming its debts, and this edition is a book of a kind: a work of the summer of 1994, kept. What follows is the roll of the people and the rooms the work and its survival are owed to, each thanked for the specific thing they did. The sources behind every name here stand in the bibliography.

Judy Malloy

First, and by the most, Judy Malloy. She originated and orchestrated Scibe: in July 1994 she was seriously injured, and on August 17, confined to bed in Tempe, Arizona, on a laptop lent by Xerox PARC's Computer Science Lab, she began the work — asking her colleagues on The WELL and in ARTS WIRE's Interactive Art Conference for windows onto their worlds, and writing back the hospital, the pain, and the poems they were sent. The collaboration that followed was, in this edition's own words, hoped for but not asked for, and it ran on two systems at once because she ran it on two systems at once. Before Scibe she had written and programmed Uncle Roger (1986); she served on ARTS WIRE's staff from 1993 and edited its newsletter from 1996 to 2004. And the work's survival is hers in the most literal way: the original still stands at her pages on The WELL, people.well.com/user/jmalloy/scibe/story.html, and this edition is based on that keeping.

The seven who joined her

The work's copyright line names eight authors. After Malloy: Tom Igoe, Chris Abraham, Tim Collins, Anna Couey, Valerie Gardiner, Joseph Wilson, and Doug Cohen. The debt to the seven is the work itself: each answered her asking with writing of their own, and the work is what their answers made. Anna Couey's debt is double — she stands in the copyright line, and in 1993 she co-founded, with Malloy, the Interactive Art Conference the work was partly written in. Chris Abraham stands third in the line, as printed; the work index counts eighteen lexias under his name.

The rooms and their keepers

ARTS WIRE, a program of the New York Foundation for the Arts, ran from 1992 to 2002. It was proposed by Anne Focke, backed by the Foundation's executive director Ted Berger, and directed from 1995 by Joe Matuzak; its staff met as a virtual office across five cities, and its conferences were rooms this work was written in. The thanks run to the institution, and to the three names the record keeps for it.

The WELL, founded in 1985 by Stewart Brand and Larry Brilliant, was the work's second room and has been its keeper ever since. When Salon sought a buyer in September 2012, the members pledged to save the community, and it passed to The WELL Group, Inc., owned by eleven longtime members; the conferences continue, and Scibe still stands where it stood. A work kept readable for thirty years is kept by somebody, year after year; the thanks are owed to the hosts and members who did that keeping, and they are recorded here.

The village, and the machinery

Scibe's writers crossed between The WELL and The Meta Network, and the village this edition's wiki rolls out is thanked as a whole. At its head, in memory: Frank L. Burns (1939–2003), who founded The Meta Network in 1983 and was president of Metasystems Design Group. His son Scott Burns kept TMN's machines — the editor remembers him as its system administrator and mechanic, living on site — and the Caucus 2.7 Menu User's Guide runs its worked example on his accounts, “the elder” and “the notsoelder.”

The machinery the village talked through was other people's work. Charles Roth wrote Caucus, and in February 1995, in about a month, wrote CML, its web language. Behind Caucus stands Robert Parnes's CONFER, written at the University of Michigan in 1975; beside it, Marcus D. Watts's PicoSpan ran The WELL's rooms. Tom Mandel and Screen Porch carried Caucus onto the web, and the stewards of CaucusCare keep the software and its archive now. Lisa Kimball — partner at Metasystems Design Group from 1984, founder of the Electronic Networking Association in 1985 — interviewed Roth in 2004, inside his own software, and published the interview at caucuscare.com; this edition's Caucus entry rests on it, and the debt is named where it is owed. The wider roll — Carl Loeffler and Fred Truck of the Art Com Electronic Network (1986); Pauline Oliveros, David Mahler, and Douglas Cohen of NewMusNet; David Green, who edited Arts Wire Current; Reiko Goto, Tim Collins's collaborator — stands in full in the village entry, and the thanks here extend to all of it, and to the hosts, named and unnamed, who greeted, mediated, and kept the talk they tended: as the village entry closes, the works were written in rooms somebody kept.

The chronicler

Howard Rheingold's The Virtual Community (1993) named the era while it was still happening. His verdict on Lisa Kimball stands in this edition's village entry — “hard to find any significant CMC system in the world that doesn't have a contribution from her” — and it is the measure the edition uses for her. For the naming, and for the measure, thanks.

Readers, listeners, and the open file

Last, the readers and the listeners. The work was written to be read; it has been read at Malloy's WELL pages for thirty years, and in this edition it can also be heard, read aloud in the readings. Thank you for reading, and for listening.

The file is not closed. Corrections, and news of surviving paper, are welcome at the colophon's address, cabraham@gmail.com, where the edition's good-faith terms stand; a misstated debt on this page will be corrected and credited like any other error in the edition.
